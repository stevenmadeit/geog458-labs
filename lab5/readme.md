Elliott Bay Vessel Traffic, Seattle (July 2–4, 2024)


1. Study area and dates

I chose Elliott Bay and the central Seattle waterfront in Washington. The drawn box returned by AccessAIS is [-122.451, 47.564, -122.327, 47.637], which covers about 29 square miles. I used data from July 2, 2024 to July 4, 2024. AccessAIS timestamps are in UTC, and Seattle was on Pacific Daylight Time during this time period, so the data actually runs from 5:00 PM PDT on July 1 to 4:59 PM PDT on July 4, 2024. This means the evening of July 4 is not in the dataset, and the map's clock, which shows Seattle local time, shows that. The data come from NOAA's AccessAIS. (https://hub.marinecadastre.gov/pages/vesseltraffic). I chose this area because the AccessAIS vessel density layer showed that Elliott Bay was one of the most heavily trafficked spots in the region. The bay holds the downtown ferry terminal, the container terminals around Harbor Island, and the cruise ships, so many different kinds of vessels share a small stretch of water. That mix really made it a good place to compare vessel types and see how their movements differ. I chose these dates because early July is a busy season for ferries, cruise ships, and any recreational boating, and the time span includes the daytime of the July 4 holiday, which might change when and where boats appear. Three days is also short enough to keep the data small while still showing two full local days, plus partial days on each end. To keep the dataset manageable, I drew a small area of interest instead of using all of Puget Sound, limited the request to three days, and checked the estimated size before submitting the order. The order came to about 0.02 GB, way under the 2 GB limit. I then converted the CSV to JSON in the class Colab notebook, which groups the reports by vessel and stores time as elapsed seconds from the UTC origin.

2. Interpretation

The observations below come from looking at the animation and from counting vessels. A vessel is moving if in any given hour, if at least one of its reports in that hour had a speed over ground of 2 knots or more it is considered a vessel that is moving. All times are Seattle local time. The dataset contains 429 vessels, and the Other group dominates, 392 vessels, compared with 21 passenger vessels, 15 cargo vessels, and just 1 tanker. Within Other, most are recreational, 228 pleasure craft and 87 sailing vessels, followed by 25 tugs and 19 towing vessels. The filters look very different from each other. Other fills the bay with short, scattered trails, Passenger shows a small number of long and repeated paths, and Cargo shows a few long straight transits. The Tanker filter is nearly empty because only one tanker, the GLOBAL PROVIDER, appears, between about 8:00 PM on July 3 and 6:30 AM on July 4. A lot of the vessels are also not moving at all. Only about 2.6 percent of pleasure craft reports show a speed of 2 knots or more, so most of them are tied up in marinas and appear as dots rather than trails. Two of the 21 passenger paths, WSF CATHLAMET and EMERALD STAR, never moved, so only 19 passenger vessels actually draw trails. The clearest repeated routes belong to the passenger filter. Three Washington State Ferries vessels, WSF TACOMA, WSF CHIMACUM, and WSF WALLA WALLA, each have over 1,200 reports spanning the full width of the study area, from about -122.451 longitude near the western edge of the box to about -122.340 near the Seattle waterfront, along a narrow band of latitudes between roughly 47.58 and 47.61. This produces repeated back-and-forth lines between the downtown terminal and the west side of the bay. Sightseeing and tour boats such as SPIRIT OF SEATTLE, SALLY FOX, and SALISH EXPLORER cover a wider area, including the northern waterfront and the area around Harbor Island. Cargo vessels definitely behave differently. Container ships like NACC POROS, MATSON KODIAK, and YM MUTUALITY have only 13 to 39 reports, all moving, and all within a window of 10 to 45 minutes, which shows that they pass through the study area rather than staying in it. Others, like WF FILIPPOS and MANHASSET QUEEN , stay near one berth the entire time and barely draw a trail. Passenger traffic follows a clear daily rhythm or pattern. On both July 2 and July 3, no more than one passenger vessel is moving between 2:00 and 4:59 AM, the count is rising from 5:00 AM, and it peaks in the afternoon at 10 or 11 moving vessels between about noon and 5:00 PM before falling to 3 or fewer after 10:00 PM. The three Washington State Ferries vessels in particular show up as 2 or 3 moving ferries in almost every hour from 5:00 AM to 10:00 PM on both days and none from 2:00 to 4:59 AM. Recreational traffic grows over the three days and peaks on July 4. The number of Other vessels moving in the same hour (1:00 to 1:59 PM) was 14 on July 2, 20 on July 3, and 38 on July 4, and on July 4 more than 20 Other vessels were moving in every hour from 10:00 AM until the data ends at 4:59 PM. Another time pattern involves the cruise ships. CARNIVAL SPIRIT is moving only on July 2, from 5:15 AM to 4:06 PM, and CARNIVAL LUMINOSA only on July 4, from 5:19 AM to 4:13 PM. Each one has movement in the morning and another in the afternoon rather than steady activity throughout the day. Possible explanations, the overnight gap and the steady daytime presence of the three Washington State Ferries vessels are consistent with a scheduled daily service that pauses overnight. The rise in recreational traffic on July 4 may reflect the holiday and good summer weather, but the data cannot tell why individual boats went out. The morning and afternoon movement of the two Carnival ships might reflect arrival in the morning and departure in the afternoon, which is a common cruise schedule, and the Port of Seattle's cruise information could be used to confirm it. WSF CATHLAMET stays at one position for all 3,751 of its reports, which may mean it was laid up or in maintenance near Harbor Island, but that is only a guess I have. Paths connect point observations, so they do not show the true movement across long gaps in AIS reports. Vessels that are stationary or have only one report are not drawn as visible trails. Only vessels that transmit AIS are included, so small boats without any transponders are missing from the map. Finally, the dataset ends at 4:59 PM on July 4, so I cannot say anything about the holiday evening.

3. Feature proposal for a potential user

The potential user is a harbor operations planner at the Port of Seattle who needs to understand which kinds of vessels use Elliott Bay at different times of day, and in particular how ferry, cargo, and recreational traffic overlap during busy periods. To think about this need, I reviewed the deck.gl interactivity guide, which explains how picking lets a user hover over or click a rendered object and receive that object's data. That guide led me to propose a click-to-inspect type of feature for vessel trips. In the current map, a viewer can see a trail but cannot tell which vessel it belongs to. With picking, clicking a trail would open a tooltip showing the vessel's name, MMSI, vessel type, and the times of its first and last reports, and the vessel that is selectued, its full path would be highlighted while the others dim. This would let the user follow one vessel through the day, for example to count how many round trips a particular ferry made. For the planner, this would make it possible to check observed ferry traffic against the published schedule and to quickly identify an unfamiliar vessel that appears in a busy lane, without searching the data by hand.
